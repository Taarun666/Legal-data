# Plain-English Legal Drafting Rulebook

## Governing principle

**Do:** Make the reader's job easy. State exactly what the document means in ordinary, precise, speakable English.

**Do not:** Mistake traditional legal language, length, or formality for precision.

## 1. Plan before writing

### Do

- Have a real point to make; think it through before drafting.
- Identify the reader, their decision, and the practical action they must take.
- Use a nonlinear outline: collect ideas, group related points, order them, then draft.
- Put facts in chronological order unless another order gives the reader a clearer understanding.
- Keep related material together.
- Plan the beginning, middle, and end before writing the middle.
- Use a clear first-page statement of the real issue - the “deep issue” - including the legally significant facts and the requested conclusion.

### Do not

- Begin with a template or boilerplate before you understand the transaction or dispute.
- Present facts in the order you learned them if chronology is clearer.
- Organise a document around the drafter's research process instead of the reader's needs.
- Conceal the controlling question until later pages.

## 2. Build a clear document structure

### Do

- Divide a document into logical sections and subsections.
- Use informative headings that tell the reader the point of the section.
- Put the more important before the less important.
- Put broadly applicable rules before narrow rules.
- Put rules before exceptions.
- Make each section reasonably self-contained so the reader does not have to keep flipping between provisions.
- Use a table of contents for a long document.
- Put the answer or key conclusion in the first paragraph when the format permits.
- Summarise the essential point; give detail only to the extent it matters.
- Start each paragraph with a topic sentence.
- Bridge between paragraphs so the reader sees why the next point follows.
- Use signposts such as “first,” “however,” “therefore,” and meaningful headings to orient the reader.
- Keep paragraphs generally short, while varying their length naturally.

### Do not

- Use vague headings such as “Miscellaneous” or “Discussion” where a meaningful proposition is possible.
- Bury the core obligation, remedy, limitation, or exception in a later clause.
- Put an exception before the rule it qualifies.
- Force the reader to reconstruct the document's logical order.
- Over-particularise with facts, cases, or background that do not change the conclusion.
- Write long, unbroken paragraphs that conceal several distinct points.

## 3. Write sentences that work

### Do

- Omit needless words.
- Aim for an average sentence length of about 20 words; use longer sentences only when their structure remains easy to follow.
- Keep subject, verb, and object close together, preferably near the beginning.
- Use active voice where it identifies the actor and clarifies responsibility.
- Use parallel phrasing for parallel ideas, especially in lists and coordinated clauses.
- Use one clear negative when needed, and state the affirmative version when it is clearer.
- End sentences with the important information where possible.
- Make every provision speakable: read it aloud as a test.

### Do not

- Put long introductory phrases between the subject and verb.
- Stack multiple negatives.
- Use passive voice to hide who must act, who has discretion, or who bears a responsibility.
- Mix grammatical forms in a list: keep verbs with verbs, nouns with nouns, and conditions with conditions.
- End a sentence with a weak, predictable, or redundant phrase.
- Write a sentence that cannot be read aloud comfortably in one natural breath.

## 4. Choose plain and precise words

### Do

- Prefer familiar words when they are equally accurate.
- Use strong, specific verbs; minimise “is,” “are,” “was,” and “were” when a real action verb will do.
- Turn abstract “-ion” nouns back into verbs where possible: “The buyer must notify the seller,” not “The buyer must provide notification to the seller.”
- Shorten wordy phrases, especially strings built around “of.”
- Use the parties' actual names when possible.
- Use a shortened party name only when it is necessary and genuinely helps readability.
- Use a defined term consistently once it is needed.
- Use terms that an informed ordinary reader can understand.

### Do not

- Use simplifiable jargon, Latin, archaisms, or inflated words.
- Use doublets and triplets such as “null and void,” “cease and desist,” or “terms and conditions” unless each term adds a distinct legal effect.
- Invent or overuse acronyms.
- Use parenthetical shorthand names by habit.
- Use vague nouns and weak verbs when a concrete verb explains the action.
- Use synonyms for the same defined concept merely for stylistic variation; consistency beats elegance in legal drafting.

## 5. Contract and instrument drafting rules

### Do

- Draft for the actual people who will administer, perform, rely on, and sign the document.
- State the parties' main obligations first.
- Put a clear rule first, then state its exceptions and qualifications.
- Use a separate, parallel provision for each distinct item in an enumeration.
- Place a list of subparts at the end of the sentence that introduces it.
- Use headings and numbered clauses to make later reference easy.
- Use “must” for an obligation imposed by the document.
- Use “will” for a future fact or a stated consequence.
- Use “may” only to grant discretion or permission.
- Use “and” only when all linked items are required; use “or” only when alternatives are intended; rewrite if both are meant in different respects.
- Prefer the singular when it makes the provision cleaner and does not change the meaning.
- Use numerals for amounts and dates where appropriate, and choose one consistent numerical style.
- Include a form provision only if you understand its purpose, operation, and risk allocation.

### Do not

- Draft for a hypothetical court at the expense of the parties who must live by the document.
- Use “shall.” Replace it with “must,” “will,” “may,” or the present tense according to the intended meaning.
- Use a proviso (“provided that”) to hide an exception. State the rule, then begin a new sentence or clause for the exception.
- Use “and/or.” Choose the intended connector or rewrite the sentence to state the alternatives precisely.
- Place a long enumeration at the beginning or middle of a sentence.
- Create a large definitions section at the front of a document. Minimise definitions; if many are unavoidable, place them in a schedule or dedicated section after the substantive provisions.
- Repeat both words and numerals for amounts merely as a ritual unless a governing rule requires it.
- Retain copied boilerplate you cannot explain, even if it appears in a respected precedent.

## 6. Persuasive and analytical writing

### Do

- State the answer early and explain the route to it.
- Summarise holdings and facts relevant to the issue; avoid case-by-case retellings where synthesis will do.
- Weave a quotation into your sentence, explain why it matters, and use only the words needed.
- Put citations where they support the proposition without breaking the reader's flow; use footnotes where a citation system and audience support that choice.
- Deal directly and fairly with counterarguments; explain why they do not change the outcome.
- Use a civil, confident, accurate tone.

### Do not

- Begin by withholding the answer to create artificial suspense.
- Let citations fragment every sentence.
- Drop block quotations into the document without a lead-in, explanation, or link to the argument.
- Ignore the strongest contrary authority or opposing argument.
- Overstate a proposition, distort a fact, or call something “clear” instead of proving it.

## 7. Design for readability

### Do

- Use a readable typeface and a legible size.
- Create meaningful white space around sections, paragraphs, lists, and headings.
- Use bullets or numbered lists to highlight genuine sets of information.
- Use typography consistently and purposefully.
- Give long documents a navigable table of contents.

### Do not

- Use all capitals for ordinary text.
- Capitalise every significant word in headings merely as a style habit; use a consistent, readable heading style.
- Use tiny type, dense pages, or visual clutter to make a long document appear shorter.
- Use bullets where a simple sentence is clearer.

## 8. Edit systematically

### Do

- Invite constructive criticism and treat it as evidence about the reader's experience.
- Edit one kind of problem at a time instead of trying to correct everything in one pass.
- Run a structural pass: order, headings, deep issue, summaries, topic sentences, transitions, and counterarguments.
- Run a sentence pass: length, active voice, subject-verb-object order, parallelism, negatives, and endings.
- Run a word pass: jargon, weak verbs, nominalisations, wordy phrases, doublets, undefined terms, and unnecessary acronyms.
- Run a citation and quotation pass: relevance, placement, accuracy, integration, and readability.
- Run a proofing pass: grammar, punctuation, spelling, numbering, cross-references, defined terms, facts, dates, figures, and formatting.
- Keep reliable dictionaries of usage and style guides close by; verify disputed language questions rather than relying on memory.
- Observe what you dislike as a reader and identify the feature causing that reaction.

### Do not

- Assume a spell-checker catches every error.
- Accept a grammar rule merely because you half remember it from school.
- Edit only the visible surface when the real defect is structure or missing reasoning.
- Treat feedback as a reason to defend the draft rather than improve it.
- Ignore reader frustration - rereading, losing the thread, or asking “who must do what?” are signs the writing needs work.

## Final pre-send test

### Do

- Confirm that the reader can identify the issue, answer, key obligation, and next action quickly.
- Confirm that each defined term, cross-reference, number, date, and obligation is accurate and consistent.
- Confirm that each exception follows a clear rule.
- Confirm that every clause is explainable in plain English.
- Read the document aloud, then read it once as the business reader rather than the drafter.

### Do not

- Send a document because it “sounds legal.”
- Let boilerplate, jargon, or cautious over-drafting obscure the parties' actual deal.
- Assume clarity and precision are competing goals: clear drafting is usually more precise.

## One-page operating principle

**Write for the person who must use the document. Put the main rule first. Use plain words, direct verbs, short organised sentences, and visible structure. Say what each party must, may, and will do. Cut anything you cannot explain. Then edit until the reader can act without guessing.**
