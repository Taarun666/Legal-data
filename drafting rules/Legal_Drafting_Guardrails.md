# Legal Drafting Guardrails

A merged, practical rulebook combining *The Lawyer's Guide to Writing Well* (Goldstein & Lieberman) and *Legal Writing in Plain English* (Garner), with one deliberate house-style override described in Section 0. Sections 1-7 otherwise follow the source rulebooks without modification.

## 0. House style override

**Do:** Draft in formal legal language and passive voice as the default register, including for operative provisions and statements of obligation or fact (e.g., "The deposit shall be returned by the Landlord" rather than "The Landlord shall return the deposit").

**Do not:** Treat this override as licence to abandon the structural, organisational, accuracy, or consistency rules below — it changes voice and register only.

**Note:** Both source rulebooks recommend the opposite — active voice and plain English — as their general rule. This override supersedes that recommendation for this house style, deliberately and by instruction.

## 1. Before drafting

### Do

- Identify the precise objective: the result, decision, or action the reader must reach.
- Identify the reader, their knowledge level, and the action they must take next.
- Identify the parties and the transaction or dispute by name.
- Form a provisional theory or position before researching; state it in one or two sentences.
- Research to test, refine, or disprove that theory, not to collect quotations.
- Rank provisions, issues, and authorities by importance; mark what can be cut first.

### Do not

- Begin with a template, boilerplate, or formal outline before the transaction or dispute is understood.
- Assume the purpose of the document; ask a focused question if it is unclear.
- Research every issue to the same depth.
- Conceal the controlling question or main obligation until later in the document.

## 2. Structure

### Do

- Organise by the reader's decision path, not by the order material was found or drafted.
- State the answer, conclusion, or main obligation in the first paragraph where the format allows.
- State the parties' main obligations first; state the rule before its exceptions.
- Use informative headings that state the point of the section, not vague labels such as "Miscellaneous" or "Discussion."
- Use a separate, parallel clause or sub-clause for each distinct item in an enumeration.
- Place a list of sub-parts at the end of the sentence that introduces it.
- Make each section reasonably self-contained.
- Begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; keep one connected subject in each paragraph.
- Build a clear transition between each section, paragraph, and sentence.
- Test the structure by reading only the lead, headings, topic sentences, and conclusion.

### Do not

- Organise a document as a chronological research diary or case-by-case digest.
- Use headings that conceal the conclusion or main obligation.
- Put an exception before the rule it qualifies.
- Bury a critical qualification, exception, or conclusion in a footnote or a later clause.
- Write long, unbroken paragraphs that conceal several distinct points.

## 3. Sentence and clause construction

### Do

- Use passive voice as the default, consistent with the house-style override in Section 0.
- Keep each sentence to one governing thought; split a sentence that loses its centre under stacked clauses.
- Keep parallel items in the same grammatical form.
- Use "must" for an obligation, "will" for a future fact or stated consequence, and "may" only to grant discretion or permission.
- Use "and" only when every linked item is required; use "or" only when the items are true alternatives.
- State an affirmative proposition rather than a negative one where both are available.
- Keep related words close together; place modifiers next to what they modify.

### Do not

- Use "shall."
- Use "and/or"; choose the intended connector or rewrite the sentence.
- Use a "provided that" proviso to bury an exception; state the rule, then state the exception in its own sentence or clause.
- Stack prepositional phrases, subordinate clauses, and qualifications until the sentence loses its centre.
- Create dangling participles, misplaced modifiers, or unclear pronoun references.
- Break parallel structure in a list or pair joined by "and," "or," or "but."

## 4. Word choice

### Do

- Use formal legal terminology deliberately and consistently, consistent with the house-style override in Section 0.
- Define each technical or defined term once, then use it consistently.
- Use the parties' actual names, or one clearly defined shortened name, consistently throughout.
- Use numerals for amounts and dates, with one consistent numerical style.
- Verify every cross-reference, defined term, number, and date for consistency.

### Do not

- Use doublets or triplets ("null and void," "terms and conditions") unless each term adds a distinct legal effect.
- Vary the term used for a single defined concept for stylistic reasons.
- Invent or overuse acronyms.
- Retain copied boilerplate that cannot be explained, even if it appears in a precedent.

## 5. Document formatting guardrails

### Do

- Place each point, clause, obligation, or list item on its own line; never run multiple points together within one paragraph or sentence.
- Apply the same spacing between every point throughout the document — identical space-after (or blank-line) spacing between every clause, sub-clause, or list item, with no variation between sections.
- Set paragraph line spacing between 1.25 and 1.5 for all body text.
- Use numbered or lettered clauses for anything the document will later cross-reference.
- Apply formatting as an explicit paragraph setting (space-after, line spacing), not as a visual approximation, when drafting in a word processor.

### Do not

- Compress multiple points onto a single line or merge them into one paragraph.
- Use inconsistent spacing between points within the same document.
- Use a line spacing outside the 1.25-1.5 range for body text.

## 6. Revision passes (run separately, in this order)

1. **Structure** — check section order, the lead, the conclusion, topic sentences, and transitions before touching language.
2. **Length** — remove unnecessary issues, repetition, and surplus citations; then trim and split long sentences.
3. **Clarity** — remove vague wording, nominalisations, and ambiguous modifiers; confirm each key term's meaning. Passive voice is retained under Section 0 and is not treated as a defect in this pass.
4. **Continuity** — from a clean copy, confirm first references, defined terms, cross-references, and factual consistency.
5. **Proofreading and formatting** — check grammar, punctuation, and numbering; confirm the point-per-line, consistent-spacing, and 1.25-1.5 line-spacing rules in Section 5 were applied throughout.

## 7. Final pre-send checklist

### Do

- Confirm the reader can state the main obligation after the first paragraph or clause.
- Confirm every clause has a clear actor and action, even when expressed in passive voice.
- Confirm every defined term, cross-reference, number, and date is accurate and consistent.
- Confirm every point sits on its own line, spacing between points is consistent, and line spacing falls within 1.25-1.5.
- Confirm every exception follows the rule it qualifies, rather than preceding or burying it.

### Do not

- Send a document because it "sounds legal" without checking it against this list.
- Assume an earlier correction did not create a new inconsistency elsewhere.
- Leave the final formatting check to someone else.
