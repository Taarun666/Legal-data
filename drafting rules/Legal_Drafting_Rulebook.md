# Legal Drafting Rulebook

## The governing test

**Do:** Write so that a busy reader can identify the answer, the reason, and the required action on the first read.

**Do not:** Treat legal writing as a display of research, vocabulary, or formality.

## 1. Before you draft

### Do

- Identify the precise objective: what result, decision, or action should the reader reach?
- Identify the reader, their knowledge level, and what they need to do next.
- Form a provisional theory before researching. State it in one or two sentences.
- Research to test, refine, or disprove that theory - not to collect quotations.
- Start early enough for reflection, reconsideration, revision, and proofreading.
- Keep research notes organised by issue, authority, fact, and practical significance.
- Make a rough outline while researching; make a formal outline only when the argument has taken shape.
- Set a word or page budget before drafting and allocate space by importance.
- Rank authorities and arguments by importance. Mark material that can be cut first.

### Do not

- Begin with a polished introduction, a formal outline, or a template before you understand the problem.
- Assume the assignment’s purpose. Ask a focused question if the goal is unclear.
- Research every possible issue equally.
- Copy quotations or citations without reading and understanding their source.
- Leave drafting until the deadline and thereby sacrifice editing.
- Mistake a collection of case summaries for legal analysis.

## 2. The drafting process

### Do

- Treat the first draft as problem-solving on paper; allow it to be imperfect.
- Separate composing from editing: compose early; edit late.
- Use this workflow for substantial work:
  1. Develop a theory.
  2. Research and take notes.
  3. Build a rough outline.
  4. Reassess the theory.
  5. Create a formal outline.
  6. Draft.
  7. Reorganise.
  8. Rewrite.
  9. Edit in passes.
  10. Proofread.
- Revisit the theory whenever research or drafting exposes a weakness.
- Leave time between drafting and editing so that you can read the work as the audience would.

### Do not

- Edit every sentence while trying to discover the argument.
- Fall in love with the first theory, outline, or phrasing.
- Assume a complete first draft communicates clearly merely because it is complete.
- Treat writer’s block as a wording problem when it actually signals missing analysis or research.

## 3. Lead with the answer

### Do

- Start with the conclusion, recommendation, or central proposition whenever the format permits.
- In the first paragraph, identify the issue, answer, scope, and practical route through the document.
- Make the lead specific: name the parties, transaction, issue, and outcome.
- Rewrite the lead after completing the document if the argument has changed.
- End with a conclusion that confirms the result and the next step.

### Do not

- Open with throat-clearing: “I have been asked to…”, “This memorandum concerns…”, or “The issue I wish to address is…”.
- Begin with a history lesson, procedural detail, or a string of citations before stating the point.
- Make the reader hunt for your answer.
- Restate the assignment instead of giving the result.

## 4. Structure the reader’s path

### Do

- Organise by the reader’s decision path, not by the order in which you found information.
- Give each major section one clear function.
- Use informative headings that state the conclusion or point, not merely the topic.
- Use subheadings in long or complex documents to mark the route.
- Use lists for three or more parallel items, alternatives, conditions, or steps.
- Begin each paragraph with a topic sentence, usually its first sentence.
- Keep one connected topic in each paragraph.
- Build logical transitions between sections, paragraphs, and sentences.
- Test the structure by reading only the lead, headings, topic sentences, and conclusion.

### Do not

- Organise a memorandum as a chronological research diary or a case-by-case digest.
- Use headings that conceal the conclusion (“Background”, “Discussion”) when a useful proposition can be stated.
- Mix unrelated propositions in one paragraph.
- Rely on the reader to infer why one point follows another.
- Bury a critical qualification, exception, or conclusion in a footnote.

## 5. Be concise without losing substance

### Do

- Include all material issues, but give the most important ones the most space.
- Perform two cuts: first remove unnecessary sections and minor arguments; then remove redundant words and phrases.
- Prefer a short synthesis of authorities to repeated individual case descriptions.
- Use short quotations only when the exact language is unusually authoritative, vivid, or necessary.
- Introduce and explain every quotation in your own argument.
- Cite the principal, relevant authorities that you have read.
- Read sentences aloud; split or trim any sentence that makes you lose the thread or pause for breath.

### Do not

- Fill the allowed page count just because it is available.
- Include tangents, historical surveys, duplicate arguments, or research that does not advance the decision.
- Use long block quotations as a substitute for analysis.
- Use string citations to signal effort or authority.
- Cite a case you have not read or that does not materially support the proposition.
- Put substantive analysis in footnotes merely to keep it out of the main text.

## 6. Use plain, precise language

### Do

- Prefer familiar, concrete, exact words.
- Use the shortest accurate word: **use**, not **utilise**; **about**, not **with respect to**; **because**, not **due to the fact that**.
- Put the key action in a strong verb.
- Put the actor in the subject position whenever possible.
- State a proposition affirmatively when that is clearer.
- Define unavoidable technical terms once, then use them consistently.
- Check a comprehensive dictionary for a word that may have a specialised or easily confused meaning.
- Use modifiers only when they add a real, verifiable limitation.

### Do not

- Use Latinisms, legalese, bureaucratese, jargon, euphemism, or pompous substitutes for ordinary English.
- Use lawyerisms such as **aforementioned**, **hereinafter**, **whereas**, **wherein**, or **said** when a plain alternative works.
- Use empty emphatics or hedges such as **clearly**, **obviously**, **undeniably**, **without doubt**, **virtually**, or **it would appear**.
- Use cliches, corporate buzzwords, or vague abstractions.
- Qualify absolute words such as **unique**, **final**, **complete**, **identical**, or **unanimous** with “very”, “almost”, or “partly”.
- Use **alleged/allegedly** by reflex; say precisely what is disputed and by whom.

## 7. Make sentences carry the meaning

### Do

- Prefer active voice: “The tenants signed the lease.”
- Use passive voice only when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, deliberately withheld, or the recipient of the action genuinely needs emphasis.
- Replace nominalisations with verbs: “We analysed the samples,” not “We carried out an analysis of the samples.”
- Replace “there is/are/was/were” openings with a direct subject and verb.
- Keep related words close together and place modifiers next to what they modify.
- Keep parallel items in the same grammatical form.
- Use a sentence length that matches the thought; divide complex reasoning into linked sentences.
- Use “that” for restrictive clauses and “which” (usually after a comma) for non-essential information.

### Do not

- Hide action in abstract nouns followed by weak verbs such as “make”, “do”, “conduct”, or “carry out”.
- Omit the actor without a reason.
- Stack prepositional phrases, subordinate clauses, and qualifications until the sentence loses its centre.
- Use a negative when a clear affirmative statement is available.
- Create dangling participles, misplaced modifiers, unclear pronoun references, or faulty subject-verb agreement.
- Break parallel structure in a list or pair joined by “and”, “or”, “but”, or “rather than”.
- Split infinitives where a natural alternative exists; do not contort a sentence merely to avoid one.

## 8. Accuracy, grammar, and usage

### Do

- Make every first reference fully identify the person, document, case, or entity.
- Maintain consistent names, defined terms, dates, capitalisation, spelling, abbreviations, number style, and citation style.
- Verify every cross-reference, page reference, quotation, citation, defined term, number, and date.
- Distinguish words that are commonly confused: **affect/effect**, **adopt/adapt**, **allude/elude**, **alternate/alternative**, **among/between**, **disinterested/uninterested**, **tortuous/torturous**, and **venal/venial**.
- Use “whether or not” only when both outcomes are meant; otherwise use “whether”.
- Use “try to”, not “try and”.
- Use “who” for a subject and “whom” for an object.
- Use “were” for a contrary-to-fact condition where the subjunctive is required.

### Do not

- Depend on spell-checking as proofreading; it misses correctly spelled wrong words.
- Use unexplained abbreviations in ordinary text, except familiar titles and widely recognised initials.
- Use **all right** as **alright** in formal writing.
- Use **where** when you mean “when” or a condition.
- Use **admit** as a weak substitute for “said”, “stated”, or “acknowledged” unless an admission is actually intended.
- Confuse an **alternative** (a choice) with an **alternate** (a substitute or every other).

## 9. Revision protocol: edit in separate passes

### Pass 1 - Structure

**Do:** Check the sequence of major sections, the strength of the lead, the conclusion, topic sentences, and transitions. Reorder before polishing language.

**Do not:** Line-edit a structurally unsound draft.

### Pass 2 - Length

**Do:** Remove unnecessary issues, repetition, digressions, excess quotation, surplus citations, and wordy phrases. Then trim and split long sentences.

**Do not:** Protect text merely because it took time to research or write.

### Pass 3 - Clarity

**Do:** Remove legalese, vague wording, nominalisations, unnecessary passive voice, clutter, and ambiguous modifiers. Check each key word’s meaning.

**Do not:** Let formality conceal uncertainty or lack of analysis.

### Pass 4 - Continuity

**Do:** Start from a clean copy. Confirm first references, transitions, defined terms, cross-references, internal consistency, and factual continuity.

**Do not:** Assume an earlier correction did not create a new inconsistency elsewhere.

### Pass 5 - Proofreading

**Do:** Check spelling, grammar, punctuation, typography, citations, formatting, headings, numbering, and a consistent editorial style. Put the document aside, then read it once more as the intended reader.

**Do not:** Send a document directly from spell-check to signature, filing, or client delivery.

## 10. Final pre-send rules

### Do

- Ask: Can the reader state the answer after the first paragraph?
- Ask: Does every section advance the objective?
- Ask: Does every sentence have a clear actor, action, and meaning?
- Ask: Can any word, citation, quotation, footnote, or paragraph be removed without loss?
- Ask: Is every statement accurate, supported, and appropriately qualified?
- Ask: Would a busy non-specialist understand the document without a guided explanation?

### Do not

- Submit writing that is correct but difficult to navigate.
- Confuse completeness with length.
- Confuse technical language with precision.
- Leave the final read to someone else.

## One-page operating principle

**Think first. Draft freely. State the answer early. Organise around the reader’s decision. Use plain words and active verbs. Cut without mercy. Edit in passes. Proofread as if the reader will rely on every word.**
